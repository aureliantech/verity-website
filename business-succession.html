What it does

What Business Succession Planning Actually Does

Puts a real, binding plan behind a decision most owners only carry around in their head.

Identifies your successor — family, a partner, a key employee, or an outside buyer — and puts it in writing.

Covers what happens if you become disabled or pass away, not just a planned retirement.

Establishes how the business will be valued when the transition happens, so it isn't guessed at later, under stress.

Coordinates the insurance or financing that pays for the transfer, so the business isn't sold at a discount to cover it.

Makes sure the business transfers the way your will or trust says it should, not in conflict with it.

Builds in a transition period so the person taking over is actually ready to run it before you're gone.

What we watch for

Common Mistakes We Watch For

Most succession problems trace back to a plan that only ever existed in conversation. Here's what we check for, every time.

An idea in your head isn't binding, and it disappears the moment something happens to you.

Retirement isn't the only exit. A stroke or an accident can force a transition with no warning at all.

Without one, the number gets decided under stress, and partners or family often disagree on what the business is worth.
